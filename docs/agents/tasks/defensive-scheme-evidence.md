# DEFENSIVE-SCHEME-EVIDENCE

## TL;DR

- The public evidence supports a league switch rate and a few directly tracked matchup examples.
- Team and player matchup summaries can show who guarded whom over the year.
- Ball-screen coverage depends on the two players involved and can change within a game.
- Help, zone and off-ball screening need their own measurements.
- This research is for the Stage 2 coach and possession model.

## 1. Pick-and-roll coverage

A drop keeps the screener's defender closer to the basket than the screen. At-level coverage brings that defender up to contain the ball near the screen. A show or hedge sends them briefly toward the ball before they recover. A blitz commits two defenders to the ball. A switch trades assignments. Ice tries to deny a side screen and send the handler toward the sideline or baseline. Ice describes the ball defender's direction and can coexist with a dropping big. Coverage can also change after the first call. ## 2. Switching and mismatch hunting

The NBA's matchup feed measures the portion of a possession spent guarding a particular opponent. Its position groups give us a team-by-team stand-in for how unusual assignments are. A guard can start on a center, pick one up in transition, help temporarily or get there through a switch. Season totals cannot tell those paths apart. The shooting column gives effective field goal percentage, which counts three-pointers as one and a half makes. It describes shots attributed to the matchup and includes the offense's choice of when to shoot. [NBA matchup definition](https://www.nba.com/news/lu-dorts-defensive-excellence-is-pushing-okc-to-the-next-level) · [NBA matchup box score example](https://www.nba.com/game/sas-vs-nyk-0042500404/box-score?type=matchups)

| Regular season | Guards' assigned time in partial possessions against centers | Centers' assigned time in partial possessions against guards | Centers shooting against guards | Centers shooting against centers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023–24 | 5.4% | 20.9% | 63.2% | 56.1% |
| 2024–25 | 6.0% | 22.6% | 61.9% | 55.7% |
| 2025–26 | 6.3% | 24.6% | 64.8% | 56.4% |

The 2025–26 center-on-guard and guard-on-center slices had different denominators. Each is a share of that defender group's own tracked matchups. Centers' 64.8% shooting when guarded by guards still mixes rolls, transition, post-ups and other plays. The 8.4 percentage-point difference from center-on-center shooting is too broad to use as a post-switch scoring bonus. Public matchup tracking logged Alex Caruso on Nikola Jokić for 18.3 partial possessions and six shots in 2024–25, and Jalen Brunson on Victor Wembanyama for 4.1 and five in 2025–26. Those are examples of the assignment, with too few shots to estimate effectiveness for either pair.

|---|---:|---|

| Same action, 2024–25 | 1,106 or 20.4 per 100 | 1.14 per direct ending |
| Oklahoma City vs. Indiana in Games 1–2 of the 2025 Finals, Tyrese Haliburton defending the screener | 22 screens | 0.73 points per chance |
| Same two games with Myles Turner or Thomas Bryant defending the screener | 25 direct endings | 35 points, or 1.40 per direct ending |

Oklahoma City repeatedly brought Haliburton into the play. Bringing his man up to screen does not require Indiana to switch, and overall chance efficiency and direct-ending efficiency use different denominators. A team-wide rate of calling for a mismatch specifically after a switch, and its points per possession, remain unmeasured. Tom Thibodeau's Knicks offer a reminder about opponent adjustments. They ranked in the bottom six for regular-season switching during his five years in New York, then switched far more often in Game 1 against Boston in 2025. Towns picked up early fouls switching onto Jayson Tatum.
