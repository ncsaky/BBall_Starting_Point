# NBA rotations and in-game decisions, 2023–26

## TL;DR

- In 3,939 games, the typical regular-season stint lasted 8:48 for stars, 7:07 for other starters and 5:13 for other reserves.
- Teams usually closed a tight game with four starters. In the playoffs, eight regular-season rotation leaders took 81% of the minutes, up from 77% in the regular season for those teams.
- Foul trouble changed with the quarter. Other starters sat within a minute 67% of the time after a second first-quarter foul and 16% after a fourth foul in the fourth quarter or overtime.
- Coaches called a timeout after about one in five opponent runs of six, eight or ten unanswered points. The team that called timeout was about as likely to score next as the team that played on.
- Rotation depth, first-substitution timing, foul trouble and starter minutes carried over best for the same coach and team. Late fouling and quick shots for a two-for-one were better explained by the score and clock.

## 1. Stints, rest and closing lineups

A stint is uninterrupted positive game-clock time on the floor. Timeouts and quarter breaks leave the stint intact if a player stays on. Rest is also in game-clock time. A **star** here is a starter who averaged at least 30 minutes and 22 points and started at least half of his earlier appearances that season, using at least eight appearances. The **sixth man** is the reserve who appeared that night with the highest prior minutes per game, using at least five appearances. Early in a season, players without enough history fall into the ordinary starter or bench groups. These are measurement groups for tonight's rotation.

| Role | Regular-season stint median (middle half) | Playoff stint median (middle half) | Typical regular bench spell between stints |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Star | 8:48 (6:24–12:00) | 9:49 (6:47–12:49) | 3:59 |
| Other starter | 7:07 (5:04–9:29) | 6:59 (4:26–9:47) | 4:37 |
| Sixth man | 6:43 (4:04–9:57) | 5:26 (2:49–8:39) | 5:17 |
| Other bench | 5:13 (3:07–7:44) | 3:58 (2:08–6:21) | 6:52 |

In the regular season, **76% of stars** and **96% of other starters** first sat in the opening quarter. Among those who sat in that quarter, the typical exit came at **3:36** left for stars and **4:37** for other starters. Across all first exits, including those that came after the quarter, the median clock was **2:52** and **4:31** in the first. The first bench spell lasted **5:08** for stars and **6:06** for other starters. Roughly three in ten first returns came in the first quarter. Among second-quarter returns, the median return was **7:34** left for stars and **8:20** for other starters. In the playoffs, just 60% of stars first sat in the first quarter and their median first rest fell to **3:41**.

| Average minutes per player | First | Second | Third | Fourth |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Regular star | 9.7 | 7.8 | 9.8 | 7.0 |
| Regular other starter | 8.1 | 7.2 | 8.0 | 6.2 |
| Playoff star | 10.5 | 8.7 | 10.4 | 8.3 |
| Playoff other starter | 8.6 | 7.8 | 8.5 | 7.0 |

The typical team gave **10 players at least five minutes** in a regular-season game and **nine** in a playoff game. In the final five minutes of regulation, teams used an average of **6.2 distinct players** during stretches within five points, **6.8** during stretches 6–19 points apart, **7.3** when up 20 or more and **6.4** when down 20 or more. Exposure varies because teams move between margins during those five minutes.

In fourth-quarter stretches within five points and under five minutes left, starters filled **76% of floor time in the regular season** and **83% in the playoffs**, averaging each team-game equally. The exact opening five occupied **23%** and **34%** of that time. Four starters was the median in the last observed close lineup in both phases. A team's eight regular-season minutes leaders were 89% likely to appear in a given playoff game and 86% likely to log five minutes, on average. Those eight received **81.2% of playoff minutes** versus **76.7% of regular-season minutes** among playoff teams. The 2025–26 holdout gave the top eight **78.7% of playoff minutes**. Injured and traded players can be among a team's regular-season eight.

## 2. Blowouts, foul trouble and short rest

These are live margins at fixed points in the fourth quarter. The table counts the starters still on the floor. It also shows the typical time of a starter's last exit when the margin at that exit was in the corresponding band. Last exits exclude identified foul-outs and ejections and require the player to stay off through the end of the game.

| Live margin | Starters on floor at 6:00, on average | At 3:00 | At 1:00 | Median clock on last exit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Within 5 | 3.4 | 3.9 | 3.9 | 0:36 |
| Ahead 15–19 | 3.2 | 2.9 | 1.1 | 2:10 |
| Behind 15–19 | 3.2 | 2.3 | 0.9 | 3:19 |
| Ahead 20–24 | 2.5 | 1.4 | 0.3 | 3:42 |
| Behind 20–24 | 2.4 | 1.0 | 0.7 | 4:53 |
| Ahead 25 or more | 1.0 | 0.4 | 0.2 | 6:37 |
| Behind 25 or more | 1.3 | 0.8 | 0.7 | 6:48 |

At 3:00, a starter still on the floor and up 20–24 left for good during the next minute in **59%** of cases. Down 20–24, the rate was **32%**. Many trailing teams had already pulled their starters by then. For a player on the floor in a game within five, it was under **1%**. Score and time improved held-out predictions over one league-wide exit rate.

| Personal foul reached | Star sat within 1 minute | Other starter sat within 1 minute | Bench time if player returned, star / other |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Second in first quarter | 53% (342 fouls) | 67% (2,561) | 6:20 / 7:49 |
| Third before halftime | 52% (250) | 74% (2,409) | 3:21 / 3:25 |
| Fourth, quarters 1–3 | 53% (296) | 68% (2,547) | 4:54 / 7:09 |
| Fourth, fourth quarter or overtime | 12% (540) | 16% (3,371) | 2:16 / 2:39 |
| Fifth, quarters 1–3 | 82% (33) | 84% (260) | 6:58 / 7:35 |
| Fifth, fourth quarter or overtime | 24% (251) | 36% (1,825) | 2:49 / 3:12 |

On the second first-quarter foul, **41% of stars** and **54% of other starters** came out at the same whistle. The comparable rates after a first first-quarter foul were **7%** and **10%**. After a second foul and a quick exit, almost everyone returned. The older seasons give a rule of about **53%** to pull the star and **68%** to pull the other starter within a minute. For players who sat and returned, the older seasons put a typical return at **9:32 left in the second** for stars and **8:47** for other starters. The 2025–26 observations were **53% and 66%** for quick exits and **8:42 and 9:24** for median returns. With a third foul before halftime and a quick exit, the median return was the start of the third quarter for both groups in the fit and holdout. Only about half the starters pulled quickly after a fourth or fifth foul late returned, so any return-time rule needs a chance of ending the player's night. The Warriors' 2025 playoff recap gives a named exception. Steve Kerr left Stephen Curry in after a third foul with almost four minutes left in the second. [Golden State's Game 6 recap](https://www.nba.com/warriors/news/gameday-recap-20250502)

A player's time on the floor is a workload measure. In quarters 1–3 and away from the buzzer, other starters still on the floor at four, six and eight minutes into a stint exited in the next minute **7%, 19% and 27%** of the time. For stars the rates were **3%, 10% and 15%**. These patterns include planned substitutions. For the back-to-back check, the same player on the same team in the same season had to start both games, play at least 32 minutes in the first, and have at least three qualifying games with each rest gap. Across 339 qualifying player-team-seasons, the second night averaged **0.60 fewer regulation minutes** than a game following one day off. The 2025–26 comparison was **0.53 fewer minutes** across 100 player-team-seasons. The richer schedule groups improved held-out minute predictions by less than one hundredth of a minute. The comparison covers repeat starters who played both games. It cannot measure fatigue directly or identify planned rest nights.

## 3. Timeouts and opposing runs

Teams averaged **5.53 logged timeouts** in regular-season games and **5.79** in the playoffs. Regular-season averages from the first through fourth quarters were **1.09, 1.19, 1.23 and 1.95** per team. There were **0.55** per team in the final two minutes of regulation and **1.11** per team in the last two minutes of an overtime period. In the final two minutes of regulation, trailing teams called 2,477 timeouts versus 1,297 for leading teams and 271 tied. The median tied timeout came with **12 seconds** left. Leading and trailing calls both had medians of about **28 seconds**.

A run counts unanswered points within a period. An immediate response is a timeout by the team that gave up the points, before the next shot, foul, turnover or jump ball and within 10 game seconds. A scoring burst combines same-clock scores by the same team, including a free-throw trip. The next-score comparison omits cases with no subsequent score in the period.

| First crossing of opponent run | Regular-season opportunities | Immediate timeout | Removing possible mandatory calls | Team that gave up run scored next, timeout / no timeout |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 6–0 or more | 30,580 | 18.2% | 17.7% | 64.8% / 65.9% |
| 8–0 or more | 11,975 | 20.0% | 19.5% | 64.2% / 66.1% |
| 10–0 or more | 4,540 | 18.4% | 18.0% | 63.6% / 65.7% |

A run can contribute to multiple rows. Timeout frequency after any scoring burst jumped from **4.9%** on a run of zero to five points to **17–20%** at six or more. The fit supports a jump in timeout use around six points, with little evidence for a fixed eight- or ten-point trigger. The next-score comparison is descriptive. Coaches react to the circumstances, and timeouts can also come with substitutions or a new play. The feed doesn't reliably flag media timeouts. A sensitivity check flags the first two allotted timeouts where the official deadlines, team charged and approximate clock window line up. Logged challenge timeouts count in game totals and are excluded from run responses. [NBA timeout rules](https://official.nba.com/rule-no-5-scoring-and-timing/)

## 4. End-of-game tactics

Rates below are per opponent possession in the final minute of the fourth quarter or overtime, split by the margin and time when the defense entered the window. A take foul is the play-by-play's explicit personal take-foul tag. The broader check also includes ordinary non-shooting personals within five seconds of possession entry, which can include routine defense.

| Deficit on entry | 0–10 seconds | Over 10 to 24 seconds | Over 24 to 60 seconds |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 48% | 52% | 5% |
| 2–3 | 37% | 51% | 5% |
| 4–6 | 24% | 46% | 20% |
| 7–10 | 3% | 13% | 16% |
| 11 or more | 1% | <1% | 1% |

Teams down one to three and making a tagged take foul did so with a median **8–9 seconds** left. Down five or six, the medians were **18 and 19 seconds**. At 10–24 seconds and down two or three, the broader rate was **73%**, versus **51%** on the stricter tag. The fit supports a strong change with the time and score. It also shows teams down four to ten starting earlier than teams down by one possession.

**Up three.** With the opponent in possession and the defense up three, tagged take fouls occurred on **18%** of opportunities in the final 10 seconds (257 opportunities) and **10%** with over 10 to 24 seconds left (199). The broader count gave **27%** and **19%**. Tagged fouls while up three in the last 24 seconds came with a median of **6.0 seconds** left. The trained rates were **18%** and **11%**, versus **19%** and **8%** in the holdout. This was a small improvement over one rate for the full window.

**Hack-a-player.** The high-confidence pattern required two tagged take or away-from-play fouls by the same team within three minutes of game time during one period, both sending the same player to the line outside the final two minutes. The target had shot under 65% on at least 30 earlier attempts. This found **12 sequences in 3,689 regular-season games** and **nine in 250 playoff games** across eight playoff team-games. It picked up Golden State targeting Steven Adams in two quarters of 2025 Game 6 and San Antonio targeting Mitchell Robinson twice in the first quarter of 2026 Finals Game 2. The count covers repeat patterns where the feed provides both foul and recipient. A one-off hack, a foul tagged as an ordinary personal or a poor shooter without enough earlier attempts can escape the count. [Warriors–Rockets play-by-play](https://www.nba.com/game/hou-vs-gsw-0042400156/play-by-play) · [Knicks–Spurs play-by-play](https://www.nba.com/game/nyk-vs-sas-0042500402/play-by-play?period=All) · [NBA away-from-play rules](https://official.nba.com/rule-no-4-definitions/)

**Two-for-one.** A qualifying team gets the ball with 30–42 seconds remaining and takes a field-goal attempt by 25 seconds left. That leaves time for the opponent to have a possession and the first team to get it back. Intent and exact shot clock are inferred from timing.

| Time left when taking possession | First shot by 25 seconds, most situations | First shot by 25 seconds, within five in fourth quarter or overtime |
|---|---:|---:|
| 30–34 seconds | 19% | 19% |
| 35–38 seconds | 53% | 38% |
| 39–42 seconds | 65% | 43% |

In the 35–38 second band in most situations, **95%** of teams that shot early got another possession and **62%** of those attempted a second field goal. Definitions using 24 or 27 seconds instead of 25 gave the same general shape. The fitted rate used the available time and whether the team was in a close finish.

## 5. Coach differences

The coach comparison uses regular-season teams, assigning names to team-seasons with one listed head coach. A same-coach, same-team comparison uses at least 20 observations in each side of the holdout. The spread below is the middle 80% of those 2025–26 teams. Rank agreement compares the ranking estimated from the older seasons with the ranking observed in 2025–26. A value of 1.00 means the order repeated exactly. Rosters, injuries, opponent schedule and score states contribute to the team differences.

| Decision or outcome | 2025–26 spread | Rank agreement | Earlier team rate improved on league average? |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Players with at least five minutes | 9.6–10.4 | .76 | Yes |
| First starter sit, time elapsed from tip | 6:34–8:11 | .71 | Yes, average error 26 vs. 38 seconds |
| Pull starter within a minute of second foul in the first | 36–86% | .63 | Yes, average error 12 vs. 17 percentage points |
| Starter regulation minutes | 27.6–31.2 | .60 | Yes, average error 1.03 vs. 1.48 minutes |
| Quick take foul in last 24 seconds, down 1–6 | 26–51% | .40 | About even |
| Early two-for-one shot, 35–42 seconds outside a close finish | 50–63% | .40 | About even |
| Timeout at first 8–0 run crossing | 14–24% | .32 | No |
| Starter share of floor time in close finishes | 67–83% | .24 | No |

- **Steve Kerr and Tyronn Lue** show the first-shift spread. In 2025–26, Golden State's starters first sat after **6:10** on average, compared with **8:23** for the Clippers. Kerr gave **10.55** players at least five minutes per game, and Ime Udoka's Houston gave **9.44**. Houston starters averaged **33.1 regulation minutes** and Golden State's **27.3**.
- **Rick Carlisle and Kenny Atkinson** show the foul-trouble spread. Indiana made a quick substitution in **34 of 36** starter second-foul cases, compared with **10 of 32** for Cleveland. Their earlier rates were **96%** and **43%**. Counts include sustained roster and role differences.
- **Tom Thibodeau and Mike Brown** give a same-team example across a coach change. The 2024–25 Knicks gave 8.73 players at least five minutes and their starters averaged 35.3 minutes. In Brown's 2025–26 season that became 9.66 players and 31.5 minutes. The team comparison includes roster and season changes. ## 6. Held-out checks

The rules use the two earlier regular seasons. The fit takes medians for times and uses observed rates for yes/no decisions, pulled toward the league average when a group is small. The baseline predicts a single training median or league rate. Time rows use average absolute error, and chance rows use the average squared difference between the predicted probability and what occurred, also called the Brier score. Smaller errors are better.

| Rule input | 2025–26 cases | Baseline error | Fitted error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Opening stint, by starter role | 12,293 | 100.6 sec | **95.7 sec** |
| First bench spell, by starter role | 12,234 | 132.0 sec | 131.8 sec |
| Quick foul exit, by foul count, quarter and role | 7,702 | .250 | **.206** |
| Bench time after quick foul exit, conditional on return | 3,133 | 208.3 sec | **171.6 sec** |
| Return clock after second first-quarter or third first-half foul | 1,956 | 336.9 sec | **133.9 sec** |
| Permanent fourth-quarter exit in the next minute, by time and live margin | 26,182 | .068 | **.060** |
| Tonight's minutes relative to prior average, by rest and workload | 9,045 | 4.54 min | 4.53 min |
| Immediate timeout, by unanswered points | 127,516 | .0593 | **.0574** |
| Take foul when behind, by time and deficit | 3,590 | .103 | **.082** |
| Take foul when up three, by time | 162 | .122 | .119 |
| Early two-for-one shot, by clock and close finish | 4,878 | .235 | **.198** |

The fitted opening-shift medians were **9:06 elapsed for a star** and **7:41** for another starter. The held-out medians were **9:12** and **7:04**. Ordinary first bench spells varied too widely for a role-only median to help much. Conditioning on foul count and game clock helped more. These are research targets for distributions and decision rates in the Stage 2 coach. Stage 2 gameplay changes are outside this work.
