# NBA player development evidence, 2004–05 through 2025–26

## TL;DR

- Real players made their biggest gains at 19–21. Average impact peaked around 25–26, and individual seasons could move sharply in either direction.
- Among players who kept playing regular minutes, decline averaged about half an impact point a year in the early 30s and nearly a full point in the late 30s. Established stars held up better at 33–35.
- Early impact and draft slot helped predict top-25 and top-50 seasons. For the very top, the forecasts were weaker and long shots still broke through.
- About one player a year reached the top five for the first time. About two of the five spots changed hands each season, including returning players.
- Sixteen players first made the top 25 at 27–30 after appearing in the NBA at 23. Twenty-three of 150 top-ten picks fell short of both a top-50 year and three seasons of regular minutes in their first seven years.

## 1. How players improve and age

Basketball Reference's **Box Plus/Minus (BPM)** estimates how many points a player adds per 100 possessions above league average from the box score. **Value Over Replacement Player (VORP)** estimates season-long contribution above a replacement player and gives credit for more playing time. BPM is the main growth and ranking measure. VORP identifies established stars for the aging comparison and provides a check on the top five. The main panel has 11,059 player-seasons across 2,328 players from 2004–05 through 2025–26. Earlier seasons back to 1995–96 are used to identify prior entries and NBA debuts. Year labels use the season's ending year. Adding each year's mean change from age 19 onward and smoothing over three ages gives a **peak at 26**. The older fitting seasons alone also gave 26. With a 750-minute cut the peak was 25, and with 1,500 minutes it was 26. The age-24-to-27 stretch is fairly flat. The same-player comparisons avoid comparing entirely different players from one age to the next. The set of players who qualify still changes with age. At 30, 242 of 323 starting qualifiers met the cut again the next year. At 35, 54 of 84 did. That lost playing time is another part of aging beyond the change in impact per possession.

At 33–35, the stars still playing regular minutes lost about half a point less per year than other returning qualifiers in both measures. At 30–32 they declined faster in both measures. The oldest star sample includes repeat seasons from a few long careers. A blanket rule that all stars age more slowly would miss those differences.

**Held-out test.** Fit average changes in seven broad age bands using target seasons 2006–2020, then forecast each pair ending in 2021–2026. Lower average absolute error is better.

| Rule | Average absolute error across 1,211 held-out pairs |
|---|---:|
| Repeat last year's BPM | 1.37 |
| Add average change for the age band | 1.33 |
| Add average change for the age band and starting level | 1.29 |

The middle-80% band estimated from the fitting sample covered 75.9% of held-out outcomes. The decline of players grouped by a strong starting season includes regression toward their usual level and shifts in role or health. It would be a mistake to subtract that entire amount from their underlying skills every year.

**Basketball Index check.** LEBRON blends box-score evidence with how teams perform with and without a player. The 2010–2026 dashboard matched 8,731 of 8,733 rows to the public panel. Two same-name Chris Johnson rows could not be assigned confidently. The progression capture agreed with the overlapping LEBRON values within the dashboard's rounding for the middle 80% of rows. Using the same matched pairs, both measures put the smoothed peak at 26. In LEBRON, average gains at 19, 20 and 21 were +1.02, +0.79 and +0.52. The dashboard and progression capture are two views of the same Basketball Index statistic. ## 3. How fast the top of the league turns over

Rank always gives five names. To also measure how strong the best players were, take the typical fifth-place BPM in the fitting seasons, 2005–2020, as a fixed bar of roughly **+7.28 BPM**. The median season had five players above that bar. The middle 80% had three to six, and the full range was one to nine. The five highest players averaged +8.92 BPM across the study. Their season average ranged from +6.72 in 2011 to +11.08 in 2026. The exact rankings depend on the measure. Across 22 seasons, there were **22 first-time top-five players** and **41 new or returning players** in the five slots, after checking the 1996–2004 leaderboards for previous appearances. In the most recent six seasons there were four first-time players and eight new or returning players. In 2026 all five from 2025 stayed in the top five. These are league-wide rates. A typical three-year stretch can yield several debuts without needing one every season.

A spell starts when a player enters or re-enters the top five after a season outside it. Of 41 starts, 19 stayed there the next season. The chance of reaching a third consecutive season was about 39%, and the chance of reaching a fifth was about 19%, allowing for spells still in progress at the end of the panel. The 14 first-time entrants with a full decade of follow-up had a median of four total top-five seasons in that decade, including returns. LeBron James had 16 top-five seasons across the full study. Giannis Antetokounmpo had eight and Nikola Jokić had seven. Those runs matter for the game's age distribution at the top. ## 4. Late bloomers and high picks who fell short

A late bloomer is a player who appeared in the NBA at 23 and first reached the top 25 at 27–30. The stricter definition also requires that he was outside the top 100 or below the minutes cut at 23. A bust in the draft table is a top-ten pick who missed the top 50 and had fewer than three qualifying seasons in his first seven years.

|---|---:|---:|

Derrick White went from 139 minutes at 23 to his first top-25 BPM season at 29. Goran Dragić went from 116th at 23 to 19th at 27. Khris Middleton went from 61st at 23 to 16th at 28. Steve Novak also qualifies, with one particularly strong season on the rate measure. There's enough evidence to leave room for late breakouts, including ones that last only a season. ## Gaps

- Minutes and roles affect who qualifies, and both impact measures reflect the lineups and assignments a player actually had. The year-to-year spread includes shooting swings, injury, role changes and measurement noise. The evidence gives a target for simulated outcomes. It does not estimate how much variation to put in permanent ratings.
- Career exits, years without NBA minutes, and declines below the minutes cut are counted in the JSON. The change curves only measure players with enough minutes in both seasons. At older ages, the apparent decline is likely milder than the combined effects of losing playing time and declining on the court.
- The young-player odds cover players already in the NBA at each starting age. Players who debuted after that age enter the study at later ages. The seven-year window excludes newer starting cohorts and misses draft outcomes that take longer. The undrafted sample is restricted to players who made the league.
- The tiny age-38-and-older samples and the six-season turnover test give weak evidence for exact rates. Individual impacts are sensitive to the metric, especially for players in narrow roles and players with shorter seasons. The estimate uses NBA regular seasons and one approximate schedule length for the uneven 2020 season.
